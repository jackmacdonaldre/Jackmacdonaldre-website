Buy Before You Sell on the Eastside: Does It Make Sense?

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

It comes up in almost every conversation I have with move up buyers on the Eastside: Can we find the next place first, then sell? The honest answer is that it depends less on preference and more on your financial situation, your risk tolerance, and what the market is doing at the exact moment you are making the move. So let me walk through what this actually looks like in practice.

Why People Want to Buy First

The appeal is obvious. You find the right house, you move in, then you sell your current home without living through a renovation project, staging chaos, and showings at 6 PM on a Thursday. You get to take your time on both ends. For families with kids, it also avoids the double move, the temporary rental, the storage unit.

That is the dream version. The realistic version has a few complications worth thinking through carefully.

The Core Problem: Carrying Two Mortgages

Unless you are paying cash or have significant liquidity, buying before you sell usually means carrying two mortgages at once, even if only for a few months. On the Eastside, where Bellevue consistently ranks among the most expensive housing markets in the country, that is not a minor inconvenience. Carrying two mortgage payments in this market can add up to a very significant monthly expense depending on your loan balances and current rates. That is real money, and it adds up fast if your current home sits longer than expected.

Some buyers assume their current home will sell in two weeks because that is what happened to their neighbor in 2021. The market is more variable now. Some homes in Bellevue and Kirkland are still moving quickly, but price point and condition matter a lot. A home that needs work or is priced at the high end of its range may take 30 to 60 days or longer to close. Plan around that possibility, not the best case scenario.

How Lenders Look at This

Getting approved to buy while you still own your current home is not automatic. Most lenders will count your existing mortgage against your debt to income ratio unless you can show a signed purchase and sale agreement on the sale side. Some loan programs let you exclude the departing residence payment if you have enough equity and can document the pending sale, but that depends on the lender and your specific numbers.

It is worth having a direct conversation with your lender before you go under contract on anything. Ask them exactly what happens to your qualification if your current home has not sold by closing. Get that in writing if you can. I have seen buyers get surprised by this mid transaction, and it is a stressful place to be.

Bridge Loans: What They Actually Are

A bridge loan is short term financing that lets you tap into the equity in your current home to fund the down payment on the new one. You pay it off when you sell. Banks and local lenders do offer these on the Eastside, but they come with tradeoffs.

Interest rates on bridge loans are typically higher than a standard mortgage, and lenders usually want to see strong equity and clean credit. Some require that your current home be listed for sale before they will fund the bridge. The fees can be meaningful. This is a legitimate tool, but go in with your eyes open on the cost.

Contingent Offers and How Sellers Respond to Them

You can write an offer on a new home with a contingency that says the purchase depends on the sale of your current home. In a slower market, sellers sometimes accept these. In a competitive situation, a contingent offer is usually not competitive at all, especially in Bellevue, Kirkland, and Redmond where well priced homes still attract multiple offers. If the house you want is likely to have competition, a contingent offer may not get you far.

That does not mean contingent offers never work. On higher priced homes with smaller buyer pools, or on properties that have been sitting for a few weeks, sellers are often more willing to negotiate. It is very situational.

The Case for Selling First

Selling first has one real advantage that often gets underweighted: you know exactly what you have to work with. Once your home closes, you have a number. You are not estimating, not hoping, not crossing your fingers that the appraisal comes in. That clarity changes how you can negotiate on the buy side.

The tradeoff is that you may need a short term rental between closings. On the Eastside, that market is not cheap, but a month or two of rent is almost certainly less expensive than carrying two mortgages or paying points on a bridge loan. It is worth running the actual numbers before you decide the rental option is not worth it.

Some sellers also negotiate a rent back from their buyer, meaning they sell the home but stay for a set period after closing while they close on the next purchase. That can smooth the timing considerably if you structure it right upfront. The length of the rent back depends on the buyer's loan type and lender, since most conventional loan programs require the buyer to occupy the home within 60 days of closing, and some lenders cap the rent back period even shorter. Confirm the specific limit with the buyer's lender before agreeing to terms.

What Makes the Most Sense Depends on These Factors

  • How much equity you have in your current home and whether you can access it without selling
  • Whether you can qualify for the new purchase while still carrying the existing mortgage
  • How competitive the segment of the market you are buying into actually is right now
  • How quickly homes like yours typically sell in your specific neighborhood and price range
  • Your actual risk tolerance for carrying two payments if something takes longer than expected

None of these are hypothetical. They are specific to you and to the current market, which is why the answer genuinely varies. But I would push back on anyone who tells you one approach is always better without looking at your actual numbers first.

One Thing Worth Getting Right Early

Whichever direction you go, the most useful thing you can do before making any decisions is get a realistic sense of what your current home is actually worth right now, not what Zillow says, not what your neighbor got in 2022. A real comparative market analysis based on what has actually closed in your neighborhood in the last 90 days. That number anchors everything else: what you can afford, what kind of financing makes sense, how much risk you are taking on if you buy first.

The buyers who run into trouble are usually the ones who built their whole plan around an optimistic estimate and then got a different answer from the market.

Common Questions

Can I use the equity in my current Eastside home without selling it first?

Yes, through a bridge loan or a home equity line of credit, assuming you have sufficient equity and qualify. A bridge loan is specifically designed for the buy before you sell situation and lets you use your equity for the down payment on the new home. A HELOC can serve a similar purpose if you already have one in place, since many lenders will freeze or restrict draws on a HELOC once they learn your home is listed for sale or under contract, though the exact timing varies by lender. Talk to your lender before listing so you understand what is available to you.

How long does it typically take to sell a home in Bellevue right now?

It varies meaningfully by price point and condition. Well priced, well prepared homes in strong Bellevue neighborhoods can still go under contract within a week or two. Homes at the higher end of their range, or that need work, may take 30 to 60 days or longer. When you are deciding whether to buy first, plan around the slower scenario, not the fast one.

What is a rent back and how does it help with timing?

A rent back is an agreement where you sell your home but stay in it for a set period after closing, paying rent to the new owner. It can be a practical way to bridge the gap between your sale closing and your purchase closing without needing a short term rental. The actual length allowed depends on the buyer's loan type and lender. Most conventional loan programs require the buyer to occupy the home within 60 days of closing, which is the common ceiling for a rent back, and some lenders cap it shorter than that. It needs to be structured clearly in the contract upfront, and both sides should confirm the limit with the buyer's lender before agreeing to a timeline.

Is a contingent offer worth submitting on the Eastside?

It depends on the property. For a home that has been on the market for a few weeks, or a higher priced home with a smaller pool of buyers, a seller may be open to it. For a fresh listing in a competitive price range in Bellevue, Kirkland, or Redmond, a contingent offer usually will not be competitive. You need to be realistic about the likelihood of acceptance before counting on that strategy.

What happens if I buy first and my current home takes longer to sell than expected?

You carry two mortgages until it closes. At Eastside price points, that can be a significant monthly expense. If the home eventually sells below your estimate, your overall financial picture changes too. This is not a reason to never buy first, but it is a reason to stress test the scenario with real numbers before committing.

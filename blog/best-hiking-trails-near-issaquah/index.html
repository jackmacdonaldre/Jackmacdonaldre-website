Best Hiking Trails Near Issaquah, WA

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

One of the things people don't always realize when they're thinking about moving to Issaquah is just how much trail access you get right from town. We're not talking about a 45 minute drive to a trailhead. Some of these hikes start a few minutes from downtown, and others are close enough that people squeeze them in before work. That's genuinely unusual for a city this size, and it's a real part of why people stay here once they arrive.

Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park

Cougar Mountain sits just west of Issaquah and is the one a lot of residents end up exploring first. The park covers over 3,000 acres and has somewhere around 36 miles of trails, which means you can go back repeatedly and not feel like you're running the same loop. The terrain is dense second growth forest, mostly quiet and shaded, which makes it comfortable even on warmer summer days.

The Coal Creek Trail is one of the more popular entry points, following an old coal mining route through the valley. You'll see remnants of the mining history along the way, old infrastructure and interpretive signs, which gives it a slightly different feel than a purely natural trail. If you want a longer outing, the Anti, Aircraft Peak trail takes you to one of the higher viewpoints in the park, though the views are partially obstructed by trees depending on the season.

Parking at Cougar Mountain can get tight on weekend mornings, particularly at the Red Town Trailhead off Lakemont Boulevard. Going early or on a weekday makes a noticeable difference.

Tiger Mountain State Forest

Tiger Mountain sits just southeast of Issaquah and is a completely different experience than Cougar Mountain. This is a working state forest, so the trails share space with active timber management in some areas. The tradeoff is that it's less manicured and more rugged, and if you're looking for something with a real climb to it, Tiger delivers in a way Cougar doesn't.

West Tiger 3 is probably the most well known hike in the entire area. It's about five miles round trip with roughly 2,100 feet of elevation gain, which is a serious morning workout by any measure. The payoff is a clear, day view across the Puget Sound lowlands that's worth every step. The trailhead is right off I 90 at Exit 20, which makes it almost embarrassingly accessible for a hike that demanding.

There are also lower traffic options on Tiger if you want something quieter. The Tradition Lake Loop is a short, flat walk that's good for families with younger kids. The Preston Railroad Grade follows an old switchbacking railroad grade on the east side of Tiger. WTA rates it moderate, with about 900 feet of gain over 2.6 miles round trip, and you'll share it with mountain bikers.

One honest note about West Tiger 3 specifically: it has become very popular in recent years, and the parking lot fills fast on weekend mornings, sometimes by 7:30 or 8am. If you show up at 10am on a Saturday expecting to just walk in, you'll likely be circling for a spot or parking along the road. It's worth being realistic about that.

Squak Mountain State Park

Squak Mountain doesn't get as much attention as its neighbors, which is actually a point in its favor. It sits between Cougar Mountain and Tiger Mountain geographically, and it's consistently less crowded than either. The trail network is smaller, about 13 miles of hiking trail plus 6 miles of trail shared with horses, according to State Parks, but the forest is beautiful and the Central Peak Trail gives you a decent summit experience without the intensity of West Tiger.

The Bullitt Fireplace Trail is a nice out and back hike that leads to an old stone fireplace, all that remains of a cabin that once stood there. It's a small thing but it makes the destination feel like an actual destination rather than just a turnaround point on a ridge.

Access to Squak Mountain is a little less obvious than the other two parks. The main trailhead off May Valley Road is the easiest starting point, but signage in the area is minimal and first time visitors sometimes end up on adjacent streets looking for parking. Looking it up beforehand saves some frustration.

The Issaquah Alps as a System

Cougar Mountain, Tiger Mountain, and Squak Mountain are the best known of the Issaquah Alps, a name local hiking advocate Harvey Manning coined in the late 1970s for the foothills around Issaquah, which also include Taylor Mountain, Rattlesnake Mountain, and Grand Ridge. Together they form a connected trail system that sits almost entirely within a 20 minute drive from downtown Issaquah. For people who hike regularly, having three distinct parks with different characters this close to home is a meaningful quality of life factor, not just a weekend novelty.

There are trail connections between the parks if you're ambitious enough to link them, though that involves crossing some roads and navigating less formal paths. Most people treat each park as its own separate outing rather than trying to string them together.

Rattlesnake Ledge

Technically in North Bend rather than Issaquah, Rattlesnake Ledge is close enough that most Issaquah residents consider it part of their regular trail rotation. It's about a 4 mile round trip with about 1,160 feet of gain, and the ledge itself offers one of the best views in the region, looking out over Rattlesnake Lake with Mount Si in the background.

The honest caveat is that Rattlesnake Ledge is one of the most crowded hikes in all of King County. On a clear weekend day it can feel like a parade. Parking at the Rattlesnake Lake trailhead doesn't require a Discover Pass, according to WTA, but this is not a hike you do for solitude. You do it for the view, and the view really is that good.

A Few Practical Details Worth Knowing

Most state forest and state park trailheads in this area require a Discover Pass, which is Washington's vehicle access permit for state recreation lands. It costs $45 per year or $10 for a day pass, plus transaction fees. Cougar Mountain is a King County Regional Park and doesn't require it. The confusion between which parks need the pass and which don't is a common source of unexpected parking tickets for people new to the area.

Trail conditions change with the seasons. Tiger Mountain in particular can be quite muddy from November through March. Waterproof footwear makes a bigger difference than most people expect if you're hiking anything with significant tree cover in the winter and spring months.

Dogs are welcome on most of these trails but leash rules vary. Cougar Mountain requires leashes. Tiger Mountain requires dogs to be on leash too, according to DNR, and so does Squak Mountain State Park. Checking the current rules before you go is worth the two minutes it takes.

Why This Actually Matters for Where You Live

I mention hiking in conversations with buyers more often than people expect, because for a certain type of person it's not an amenity, it's a deciding factor. Issaquah gives you something that most suburbs don't: real wilderness access that doesn't require a road trip. You can live in a neighborhood with good schools, a reasonable commute to Bellevue or Seattle, and still walk out your door and be on a serious trail in under ten minutes. That combination is harder to find than people realize until they start looking.

Common Questions

Do you need a Discover Pass to hike near Issaquah?

It depends on which park you're visiting. Tiger Mountain State Forest and Squak Mountain State Park are state lands that require a Discover Pass for vehicle access, which is $45 annually or $10 for a single day, plus transaction fees. Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park is managed by King County and does not require one. Rattlesnake Ledge near North Bend is not a state park, and WTA lists no pass required to park there. It's worth sorting this out before you go because the parking enforcement is real.

Which trail near Issaquah is best for families with young kids?

The Tradition Lake Loop on Tiger Mountain is a good starting point. It's a nearly level loop of about 2.9 miles with a small lake and a boardwalk overlook along the way, which gives kids something to look at besides just trees. The lower trails at Cougar Mountain are also well suited for younger hikers, particularly near the Red Town Trailhead where the paths are wide and the grade is gentle.

How hard is West Tiger 3 really?

It's a legitimate workout. About 2,100 feet of elevation gain over roughly 2.5 miles one way means you're going up steeply for most of the hike. People in reasonable shape who hike occasionally can do it, but it's not a casual stroll. The trail is also very rocky in sections, so footwear matters. Budget two to three hours for the round trip depending on your pace.

Is there anywhere close to Issaquah that's good for trail running?

The Preston Railroad Grade on Tiger Mountain follows a steadily climbing old railroad grade, though it gains about 900 feet and you'll share it with mountain bikers. The lower trails at Cougar Mountain work well too, especially for people who want a longer run without constant elevation change. West Tiger 3 is hikeable but too technical in spots to run comfortably.

When is the best time of year to hike the Issaquah Alps?

Late spring through early fall gives you the most reliable conditions. Summer weekday mornings are ideal if your schedule allows it. Winter hiking is possible and the forest can be beautiful in the rain, but Tiger Mountain especially gets muddy enough that it becomes a slog in the wet months. Rattlesnake Ledge and Squak Mountain drain a bit better and hold up reasonably well year round.

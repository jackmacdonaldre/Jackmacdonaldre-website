How Property Taxes Work in King County for Eastside Homeowners

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

One of the questions I get pretty regularly from buyers, especially people moving here from out of state, is some version of: "How do I know what my property taxes will actually be?" The number on the listing is a starting point, but it doesn't always tell the full story. Let me walk through how this actually works in King County.

Your Tax Bill Is Not Just Based on What You Pay

This is the part that surprises most people. King County property taxes are based on the assessed value that the King County Assessor assigns to your home, not the price you paid for it. Those two numbers are often different, sometimes significantly so.

The Assessor's office revalues properties on a rolling cycle. King County uses a six year physical inspection cycle with annual statistical updates, meaning your assessed value gets a full physical review every six years, with statistical adjustments applied in the years between. In practice, what this means is that assessed values often lag behind the actual market, particularly after a sharp run up in prices. If you bought a home at the peak of a hot year, your assessed value might be meaningfully lower than your purchase price, at least for a while.

The tax rate itself is applied per thousand dollars of assessed value. Levy rates vary by location and by the specific mix of taxing districts your property sits in, so the only reliable way to know what a particular home is actually paying is to look up its parcel record directly on the King County Assessor's website. That said, as a rough illustration: if your home is assessed at $900,000 and the combined levy rate in your area works out to around $8 per $1,000, your annual bill would be in the neighborhood of $7,200. Actual rates across Eastside cities and neighborhoods span a meaningful range above and below that, so treat any rule of thumb as a starting point only.

What Makes Up the Tax Rate on the Eastside

Your property tax bill isn't one single charge. It's a stack of levies from multiple taxing districts layered on top of each other. Here's what typically makes up the bill for an Eastside homeowner:

  • State school levy
  • Local school district levy (this one varies a lot by city)
  • City or county general fund
  • Fire district
  • Library district
  • Emergency medical services
  • Port of Seattle
  • Sound Transit (this has grown noticeably in recent years)

The school district levy is where you see real differences across the Eastside. Bellevue School District, Lake Washington School District, Issaquah School District, and Northshore School District each run their own levies, and those get renewed and sometimes increased by voters. When a levy passes, your bill goes up. When one doesn't get renewed, it drops. This is why two homes with the same assessed value can have meaningfully different tax bills depending on which city they sit in.

Eastside Tax Rates Compared to Seattle

People sometimes assume that because Eastside home values are high, the tax rates must be higher too. That's not quite how it works. The levy rate and the assessed value are separate things. In many parts of the Eastside, the effective tax rate per $1,000 of assessed value has actually been lower than Seattle's in recent years, partly because property values here have risen faster than the levies, which dilutes the rate.

That said, you're still writing a large check in absolute dollar terms when you own a $1.5 million home in Bellevue or Kirkland. The rate might be lower on a percentage basis, but the assessed value is high enough that the total bill is substantial.

The Senior Exemption and Other Reductions Worth Knowing

King County does offer a property tax exemption program for senior citizens and people with disabilities. If you're 61 or older, own and occupy the home, and meet the income threshold (the limits have been adjusted upward in recent years, so it's worth checking the current numbers directly with the Assessor's office), you may qualify for a significant reduction in your assessed value for tax purposes.

There's also a property tax exemption available to qualifying disabled veterans, and a separate exemption for active duty military members who are deployed. These aren't advertised loudly, and a surprising number of people who would qualify never apply. If you or someone in your household might be eligible, it's worth a conversation with the King County Assessor's office directly.

What Happens to Your Taxes After You Buy

This is a genuinely important thing to think through before closing. When a home sells, the Assessor takes note. Washington State doesn't do an automatic reassessment to purchase price the way California did before Prop 13 changed things there, but a sale is considered a change in market conditions, and your home may get reviewed more closely in the next assessment cycle.

It's also worth knowing that if you buy a home with a significantly below market assessed value because the previous owner held it for a long time, there's a reasonable chance your assessed value will climb over the next few years to get closer to current market conditions. Some buyers factor this into their long term budget planning and some don't, but you should.

During the transaction itself, property taxes are prorated at closing. The seller pays their share through the date of closing and you pick it up from there. In Washington, property taxes are paid in two installments: April 30 and October 31. Depending on when you close, you might see a sizable proration credit or charge, and your lender will likely set up an escrow account to collect your future tax payments monthly as part of your mortgage payment.

How to Look Up What a Specific Home Is Actually Paying

The King County Assessor has a public search tool at the county website where you can look up any parcel by address and see the current assessed value, the levy breakdown, and what the owner paid in taxes last year. This is worth doing on any home you're seriously considering, because the tax figure on a listing sheet can sometimes be outdated or reflect a prior year's lower assessment.

When I'm working with a buyer on a specific property, pulling the parcel detail from the Assessor's site is one of the first things I do. It tells you a lot: how recently the property was assessed, whether the assessed value is unusually far below market (which may signal it's about to catch up), and exactly which districts are charging what.

One Thing That Catches People Off Guard

Washington has no income tax, which is part of why the property tax and sales tax loads here are structured the way they are. For buyers coming from states like California or New York, the absence of income tax feels like a relief, but the property tax bills on high value Eastside homes are real and shouldn't be underestimated. On a home in the $1.5 to $2 million range in Bellevue, your annual property tax bill can easily run well into five figures, with the exact amount depending on your specific location and levy code area. The best thing you can do is look up the actual parcel data on the specific home you're interested in rather than estimating from general rates. The numbers are public, they're specific, and they'll give you a much clearer picture than any rule of thumb will.

Common Questions

Does buying a home in King County trigger an automatic reassessment to my purchase price?

No, Washington does not automatically reset assessed value to the sale price the way some other states do. However, a sale is a data point the Assessor's office pays attention to, and your property may be reviewed more carefully in the next assessment cycle. If the previous owner had a significantly below market assessed value, it's reasonable to expect the assessed value to trend upward over the next few years.

Why do two homes with the same price in different Eastside cities have different tax bills?

Because your tax bill is the sum of every taxing district your property sits within, and those districts differ by location. School district levies in particular vary between Bellevue, Lake Washington, Issaquah, and Northshore districts. City levies, fire districts, and library districts also vary. Two homes assessed at the same value in Bellevue versus Redmond can have meaningfully different total bills as a result.

When are King County property taxes due?

King County property taxes are due in two installments: the first half is due April 30 and the second half is due October 31. If you have a mortgage, your lender will typically collect a portion of your estimated annual tax bill each month through an escrow account and pay it on your behalf when it comes due.

How do I find out the current assessed value and tax amount for a specific home?

The King County Assessor's website has a public property search tool where you can look up any parcel by address. You'll see the current assessed value, the full levy breakdown by district, and the actual tax amount paid in prior years. This is more reliable than the tax figure listed on a real estate listing, which may not reflect the most recent assessment.

Is there any way to lower my property tax bill in King County?

If you're 61 or older, own and live in the home, and meet the income requirements, you may qualify for the senior citizen and disabled persons exemption program, which can significantly reduce your assessed value for tax purposes. The income thresholds have been updated in recent years, so it's worth checking current eligibility directly with the King County Assessor's office. Outside of that program, you can appeal your assessed value if you believe it's higher than what the market supports, though you'll need to provide comparable sales data to make that case.

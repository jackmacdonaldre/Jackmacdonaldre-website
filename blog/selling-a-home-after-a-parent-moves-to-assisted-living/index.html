Selling a Home After a Parent Moves to Assisted Living

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

Moving a parent into assisted living is usually a decision made in a hurry, after a fall or a hospital stay, and the house gets left exactly as it was the morning everything changed. A few weeks later the family looks up and realizes there's a home sitting empty, bills still coming, and a lot of questions nobody has had time to ask.

This is a different situation than selling after a death. Your parent is still here, still the owner in most cases, and still has feelings about the place. That changes how the sale should go.

Move first, then sell

For most families I work with, the order that works is simple: get your parent settled, then sell. Trying to show a house while someone is still living in it, especially someone with mobility or memory issues, is hard on everyone. An empty home is also easier to clean, repair and photograph.

The tradeoff is carrying costs. Taxes, insurance, utilities and maybe a mortgage keep running while the house sits. That's why it helps to set a rough timeline early: a few weeks to move belongings, a few weeks to prep, then list.

Timing the market matters too, and sometimes it isn't on your side. On an estate sale in the East Renton Highlands this year, the market cooled right as we were getting ready. There were 22 homes for sale in that price range and zip code in July, up 120% from a year earlier, and only 5 sold. I laid out three options and the risk of each, the family picked one, and it sold for $625,000. Having that conversation before listing made the slower weeks a lot less stressful.

Who signs when your parent still owns the house

If the house is in your parent's name

Your parent signs, if they're able to. If they aren't, the agent named in a power of attorney may be able to. Washington's law on this is chapter 11.125 RCW. Authority over real property includes selling it, but the document itself controls what the agent can do (RCW 11.125.270). Whether it stays in effect if your parent loses capacity depends on its wording (RCW 11.125.040). And it ends at death (RCW 11.125.100), so a sale under a power of attorney can't continue if your parent passes before closing. At that point the estate process takes over.

Title companies look closely at these documents and may ask the agent for a sworn certification (RCW 11.125.190). Get the power of attorney to your attorney and the title company early.

If the house is in a trust

If your parent put the house in a living trust, the trustee signs. Often that's your parent, with a successor trustee who steps in when they can't serve. The trust document spells out when that happens, and the title company will want to see the paperwork. An attorney should confirm who has authority before you sign a listing agreement.

Keep the empty house insured and safe

  • Call the insurance company and tell them the home is vacant. Many policies treat vacancy differently, and you want to know before something happens.
  • Keep the heat on low through the winter so pipes don't freeze.
  • Consider shutting off the main water when nobody is there, if the furnace doesn't rely on it.
  • Set a few lights on timers and keep the yard mowed so it looks lived in.
  • Have someone walk through every week or two and check for leaks.
  • Remove medications, valuables and important papers.

Sale money and the cost of care

For many families, the house is what pays for assisted living. That's exactly why the money side deserves real professional advice, not guesses from an agent or a neighbor.

Before you list, sit down with your parent's financial advisor and an elder law attorney. If your parent receives or might later need long term care benefits, the elder law attorney should weigh in before the house is sold, because the sale can affect those benefits. A CPA can talk through the tax side. My job is to get the home sold well and on a timeline that fits the plan they put together.

The emotional side

This is the part nobody plans for. Your parent may agree the house needs to go and still feel like they're losing something. A few things I've seen help:

  • Let your parent choose what comes to the new apartment first, before anyone else picks.
  • Take photos and video of the house as it was, room by room.
  • Decide as a family how much your parent wants to hear about showings and offers. Some want every detail. Some want only the closing date.
  • Bring them back for one last visit if they want it, ideally after it's cleaned and staged so it looks cared for.

One more thing. Don't empty the house all at once and send the leftovers away before your parent has settled in. People often find in the first month that they want a certain chair, a lamp, or a box of photos. Give it a few weeks before the final clean out. A short wait costs very little, and you can't get those things back once they're gone.

Common Questions

Should we sell the house before or after our parent moves to assisted living?

For most families, after. An empty home is easier to prepare and show, and your parent doesn't have to live through showings. Plan for the carrying costs while it sits.

Can I sell my parent's house with a power of attorney?

Often, if the document grants authority over real property and is still in effect. It ends if your parent dies, and the wording matters, so have an attorney and the title company review it early.

Will selling the house affect long term care benefits?

It can. Talk with an elder law attorney and your parent's financial advisor before listing if your parent receives or may need long term care benefits.

Do we need special insurance for a vacant house?

Call the insurance company and ask. Many policies treat a vacant home differently than an occupied one, and it's better to know before anything happens.

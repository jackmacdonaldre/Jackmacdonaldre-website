Talus and Squak Mountain Issaquah Neighborhood Guide

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

People ask me about Talus pretty regularly, usually because they saw a listing with a mountain view and wanted to know if the neighborhood is actually as quiet and tucked away as it looks in the photos. Short answer: yes, it really is. But there are a few things worth understanding before you decide whether that tradeoff works for your life.

Where These Two Neighborhoods Actually Sit

Talus and the Squak Mountain area are on the west side of Issaquah, literally built into the foothills. Squak Mountain State Park sits between Cougar Mountain and Tiger Mountain, and together with those parks and corridors it makes this one of the greenest corners of the entire Eastside. The homes here are not far from downtown Issaquah in terms of miles, but the feel is completely different from the denser parts of Issaquah closer to I 90.

Talus is a planned community that was annexed into Issaquah in 1999 and developed in phases from around 2000 onward, with newer construction continuing well into the 2020s. It has a distinct layout, with curving roads, pocket parks, and homes that step up the hillside. Squak Mountain is less of a branded neighborhood and more of a general area description for homes near the mountain, including some older custom builds and smaller pockets of housing that predate Talus by decades.

What the Housing Stock Looks Like

In Talus, you are looking at a mix of single family homes and townhomes built across multiple phases, with some attached product mixed in closer to the main entrance. Lot sizes tend to be modest by Issaquah standards since the terrain limits how large parcels can get before they run into steep slopes or open space. Many homes have territorial or mountain views depending on where they sit on the hillside, and that view premium is real. Buyers pay noticeably more for a home with a true unobstructed view than they do for one facing another roofline a few lots away.

Near Squak Mountain proper, the housing is more varied. You will find older ramblers, some on larger lots, some with significant tree cover. These properties tend to attract buyers who want more land or a quieter, less planned feel. The tradeoff is that some of those homes need updating and the road access can be steeper and narrower depending on where exactly you are.

The Trails Are the Real Selling Point

If you are outdoorsy, this area is legitimately excellent. Squak Mountain State Park has a trail network that most people on the Eastside have never used because it does not get the marketing that Tiger Mountain or Cougar Mountain do. That means less foot traffic, which is actually a meaningful difference if you hike regularly. Parts of Talus have direct trailhead access into Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park without driving anywhere, and the Cougar Squak corridor connects onward to Squak Mountain State Park. For some buyers that kind of proximity is worth a significant amount of money on its own.

The Issaquah Alps trail network, which connects Tiger, Squak, and Cougar, is accessible from here as well. For trail runners, hikers, or people with dogs, the proximity is hard to match anywhere else on the Eastside at this price point.

Commute: Honest Assessment

This is where Talus and Squak Mountain ask something of you. Getting to I 90 takes a few minutes longer than it would from central Issaquah or neighborhoods closer to the freeway. It is not dramatic, but if you are heading to Seattle or Bellevue during peak hours, you are starting from a position where local streets funnel toward one or two exits and traffic can stack up. On a clear morning it is fine. On a bad Friday afternoon coming back from Seattle, the last few miles home can be slow.

For people working in Redmond or the SR 520 corridor, the commute usually involves getting on I 90 westbound and then connecting north, which adds time compared to living in Sammamish or Redmond directly. Worth thinking through if that is your regular route.

Issaquah has a transit center with a Park and Ride that some Talus residents use, and there is bus service connecting to the broader King County Metro network, but this is not an area where you would realistically give up a car.

Schools

Talus and the Squak Mountain area feed into the Issaquah School District, which has a strong reputation overall. Elementary school assignments depend on where exactly your home is located, so it is worth checking the district's boundary tool directly rather than assuming based on neighborhood name alone. The district has multiple high schools including Issaquah High, Liberty High, and Skyline High, and boundaries have shifted over the years.

What Kind of Buyer This Actually Suits

Talus tends to attract people who have made a deliberate choice to prioritize outdoor access and a quieter setting over walkability or proximity to retail. There is essentially no walkable commercial area within the neighborhood itself. You will drive to get groceries, coffee, or dinner. Downtown Issaquah is only a few minutes away and has a good amount of local options, but the neighborhood itself is residential through and through.

Buyers who struggle with this are usually the ones who liked the idea of the setting but did not fully account for how car dependent daily life becomes. If that matters to you, be honest with yourself before falling for a view lot.

The buyers who tend to love it are remote workers, outdoor enthusiasts, people coming from denser West Seattle or Capitol Hill who want space without moving to Snoqualmie Valley, and families who want kids to have green space literally outside the door rather than at a park a drive away.

How Talus Compares to Nearby Issaquah Neighborhoods

Compared to Issaquah Highlands, which sits north of I 90 on the Plateau, Talus is generally less dense, has fewer retail amenities baked in, and feels more tucked away. Issaquah Highlands has a Village that residents can walk to. Talus does not have an equivalent. Highlands also has somewhat newer construction on average and a different mix of homes including more attached product and condos.

Compared to neighborhoods closer to downtown Issaquah near Gilman Boulevard or Front Street, Talus homes often command a premium for views and the park adjacency, even if the square footage or lot size is similar.

A Few Practical Things Worth Knowing

Some parts of Talus have HOA coverage, and the rules and fees vary by section. Worth reviewing before you make an offer since the restrictions on things like exterior modifications, fencing, and landscaping can matter once you are living there.

Homes on steeper lots can have drainage and retaining wall considerations that are not always obvious from a standard walkthrough. If you are seriously looking at a home on a hillside parcel, a thorough inspection matters more than it would on a flat lot in a flat neighborhood.

Wildlife is genuinely part of life here. Black bears move through the area. So do deer and coyotes. That is not a complaint from most residents, but if you have small pets or a vegetable garden, it is something to factor in.

Common Questions

Is Talus a gated community?

No, Talus is not gated. It is a planned neighborhood with open access, though portions have HOA oversight covering things like exterior appearance and landscaping standards.

Are there condos or townhomes in Talus, or is it all single family?

There is a mix. The core of Talus is single family homes, but there are attached townhome style properties closer to the neighborhood entrance and in newer phases of the community. The product type and price point vary noticeably depending on which section you are looking at.

How close is Talus to Squak Mountain trails?

Very close. Parts of Talus have direct trailhead access into Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park on foot, and the Cougar Squak corridor connects onward to Squak Mountain State Park without needing to drive. That trail connectivity is one of the things that makes this area genuinely different from most Eastside neighborhoods.

Do homes in Talus hold their value well compared to other Issaquah neighborhoods?

Generally yes, particularly homes with views or strong trail proximity. View premiums in this area are real and have held up over time. Homes without views in the more interior sections of the neighborhood tend to be priced and valued more similarly to comparable Issaquah product elsewhere.

What is the noise level like near Squak Mountain?

Genuinely quiet, especially compared to neighborhoods near I 90. You hear wildlife, wind in the trees, and the occasional neighbor, but road noise is minimal in most parts of the area. That quiet is a real draw for a lot of buyers.

Are there any flood or landslide risk considerations in this area?

Hillside properties anywhere carry some geologic considerations, and parts of the Squak Mountain foothills are no exception. King County has hazard zone maps that are worth reviewing for specific parcels, and a thorough home inspection on any hillside property should include attention to drainage, grading, and any retaining structures.

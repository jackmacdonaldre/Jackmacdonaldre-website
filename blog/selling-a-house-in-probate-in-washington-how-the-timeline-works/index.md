# Selling a House in Probate in Washington: How the Timeline Works

> The order of events when selling a house in probate in Washington: letters, nonintervention powers, court approval, the creditor window, and listing vs closing.

One of the first questions I hear from an executor is some version of "how long is this going to take?" It's a fair question. There's a house sitting empty, siblings asking for updates, and bills still showing up in the mailbox.

The honest answer is that your estate attorney sets the pace, and every estate moves differently. What I can do is lay out the order things usually happen in, so you know which steps come first and which ones run at the same time.

## Step one: the court appoints someone

Nothing happens with the house until a personal representative is appointed. That's the legal name for the person most families call the executor. If there's a will, the court issues letters testamentary to the executor it names. If there's no will, the court still appoints someone, and that person gets letters too.

Those letters are the document everyone will ask for. The title company wants them, I want a copy for the listing file, and banks usually want them before they'll talk to anyone about the decedent's accounts. Keep several copies handy.

Within 20 days of being appointed, the personal representative also has to send written notice of the appointment to the heirs and beneficiaries whose addresses are reasonably known. Your attorney usually handles that mailing, but it's worth knowing it happens early.

## The big fork: nonintervention powers or not

This one detail shapes the whole sale, so ask your attorney about it right away.

In Washington, the court can grant the personal representative nonintervention powers. The statute lays out when the court must grant them, and one of the conditions is that the estate is solvent. If the will names you as personal representative and doesn't prohibit it, that's one common path. Your attorney will know if your estate qualifies.

With nonintervention powers, RCW 11.68.090 lets the personal representative sell estate property without a court order. In practice the sale looks a lot like any other: you sign a listing agreement, accept an offer, and close through escrow.

## When the court has to approve the sale

Without those powers, the default rule is different. RCW 11.56.010 says estate property isn't sold except under an order of the court, unless the law provides otherwise. That means extra steps.

Roughly, the personal representative asks the court for permission, notice goes out, and a judge signs an order directing the sale. Once the house is sold, the personal representative files a return of sale with the court within ten days. The court can then confirm the sale after a waiting period of at least ten more days. The judge can also refuse to confirm in some situations, for example if the price looks out of line with the value and a meaningfully higher bid is available.

Buyers and their agents need to know about this up front, because it affects when they can plan to move. I put it in the listing notes so nobody is surprised halfway through escrow.

## The creditor window runs in the background

Separate from the sale, the personal representative can publish a notice to creditors. Under Washington law, that notice runs in a legal newspaper in the county once a week for three weeks in a row, and it gets filed with the court.

For most creditors, RCW 11.40.051 sets the deadline at four months after the date of first publication. Creditors who were reasonably easy to find but never got actual notice can have up to 24 months from the date of death. A creditor who misses the deadline that applies is generally barred.

Why does this matter for the house? Because some attorneys want to see where the debts land before money from the sale goes out to heirs. The house can often be listed and even sold while that window is still open. Whether the proceeds sit in the estate account until it closes is a call for your attorney.

## Listing day and closing day are two different questions

Families sometimes assume they can't do anything until probate is completely done. Usually that's not the case. Once there's an appointed personal representative with authority to sell, you can start getting the house ready and get it on the market.

Closing is where the paperwork catches up. The title company will want the letters, proof of the personal representative's authority, and in a court supervised sale, the order confirming the sale. My job during that stretch is to keep the escrow officer, the attorney and the buyer's agent on the same page so the closing date holds.

## Keeping the heirs informed

A probate sale gets tense when people feel left out. Most of that is avoidable.

* Share the timeline in writing, even a rough one, and update it when something changes.

* Send the same comps, offers and counteroffers to every heir who wants to see them.

* Explain why a decision was made, not just what it was.

* Tell people early when something is waiting on the court or the attorney, so the delay doesn't look like a stall.

On a Bothell house I sold for an estate this year, two offers came in within days. I laid them side by side for the executor, term by term, so the whole family could see why one was stronger. It had an escalation clause, and the buyer agreed to cover part of a low appraisal. That kind of plain comparison does more to keep a family together than any number of phone calls.

## What to expect on timing

I won't give you a number of months, because I'd be guessing. The pace depends on how quickly the appointment happens, whether there are nonintervention powers, what the house needs before it's ready, and what your attorney wants to see before distributing money.

The most useful thing you can do is ask your attorney two questions in the first meeting: does the personal representative have authority to sell without a court order, and is there any reason to wait before listing? Those two answers tell you which version of this timeline you're living in.

## Common Questions

### Can I list a house in probate before the estate is closed?

Usually yes. Once a personal representative is appointed and has authority to sell, the house can go on the market. Your estate attorney will tell you if there is a reason to wait in your situation.

### What are nonintervention powers in Washington?

They are powers a court can grant a personal representative so the estate can be handled without ongoing court supervision. With them, the personal representative can sell estate property without a court order.

### How long do creditors have to file claims in Washington?

When notice to creditors is published, most creditors have four months from the first publication date. Some creditors who did not get actual notice can have up to 24 months from the date of death. Your attorney will explain how it applies.

### Does the court have to approve the sale?

Only if the personal representative does not have nonintervention powers or other authority. In that case the court orders the sale and later confirms it after a return of sale is filed.

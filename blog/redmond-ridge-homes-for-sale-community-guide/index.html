Redmond Ridge: What Buyers Should Actually Know

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

A lot of buyers first hear about Redmond Ridge from a coworker who lives there and won't stop talking about it. That tracks. It's the kind of place people feel genuinely good about landing in, partly because it was built with some intention rather than just platted and sold as fast as possible. But before you start browsing listings, it's worth understanding what Redmond Ridge actually is, because it surprises some buyers in ways that affect the decision.

The Short Version of What Redmond Ridge Is

Redmond Ridge is a master planned community built out mostly in the late 1990s through the 2000s, sitting on the east side of Redmond near the Union Hill Road corridor. It's not technically inside the City of Redmond proper, it's in unincorporated King County, which matters more than people expect (more on that in a minute). The community was developed by Quadrant Homes along with several other builders in phases, so the housing stock is relatively consistent in age and quality, with single family homes, townhomes, and some attached product spread across the ridgeline.

It's a planned community in the truest sense. There are parks built into the layout, trails that connect the neighborhoods, a community center, and commercial space at Redmond Ridge East. It doesn't have the organic, grew over decades feel of somewhere like Rose Hill or Bridle Trails. Whether that's a feature or a limitation depends on what you're looking for.

The Location Math

The address says Redmond, but Redmond Ridge sits further east and uphill from downtown Redmond than the name implies. Getting to the Microsoft Redmond campus or downtown Bellevue means dropping down off the ridge and navigating SR 202 or Union Hill Road to connect to either SR 520 or I 90. That commute is real. On a good day it might be 20 minutes to Microsoft. On a bad day, especially during school pickup traffic on Union Hill, it can stretch considerably. People who work in Bellevue or downtown Seattle should honestly map their specific commute at rush hour before falling in love with a house here.

That said, for people working at companies along the SR 202 corridor or in Redmond's Overlake area, the location is genuinely fine. And remote workers or those with flexible hours largely report that it's a non issue.

Unincorporated King County: Why That Matters

Because Redmond Ridge sits in unincorporated King County rather than inside Redmond city limits, a few practical things differ from what buyers expect. Permitting goes through King County rather than the City of Redmond. Some services are county managed rather than city managed. Property tax structures can differ slightly. For most buyers this doesn't come up day to day, but if you're thinking about additions, ADUs, or anything requiring permits, it's worth knowing who you're dealing with before you buy.

There is also an HOA covering Redmond Ridge, which handles the community center, trails, and common area maintenance. HOA dues are something to verify per listing since they vary by subneighborhood within the larger development.

What the Housing Stock Looks Like

Most of the single family homes were built between roughly 1997 and 2010, which means you're looking at homes that are old enough to need some updating but young enough to avoid the bigger structural and systems concerns you'd find in 1960s or 1970s construction. Typical footprints run from around 1,800 to 3,500 square feet, with most homes on the larger side compared to what that price point would get you in closer in Redmond neighborhoods.

Lots tend to be smaller than what you'd find in somewhere like Sammamish Plateau's older developments, though some of the eastern sections have more generous setbacks and views. The construction style is generally Pacific Northwest traditional, lots of Craftsman influenced detailing, two car garages, and layouts built around the family use patterns of the early 2000s. Open kitchen to family room, formal living and dining that sometimes end up underused, primary suites upstairs.

Townhomes and attached homes exist throughout Redmond Ridge and Redmond Ridge East, and those tend to price meaningfully lower, making them an entry point for buyers who want the community amenities but can't stretch to detached pricing.

Schools

Redmond Ridge feeds into the Lake Washington School District, which is consistently one of the stronger districts in the region. Louisa May Alcott Elementary is the neighborhood school physically located in the Redmond Ridge area, and it gets good marks from families there. That said, school boundaries in LWSD shift over time and assignments depend on your specific address, so always confirm your street using the LWSD school locator at lwsd.org before making decisions based on a particular school. Middle and high school pathways run through the LWSD system, which also serves Redmond, Kirkland, and parts of Sammamish. Buyers coming from outside the area often don't realize how well regarded LWSD is compared to some neighboring districts, so that's a genuine positive here.

Trails, Parks, and Daily Life

This is where Redmond Ridge earns a lot of its reputation. The trail network built into the community is genuinely good, with paved and unpaved paths connecting the neighborhoods, looping through forested sections, and linking to the larger Redmond Ridge Open Space. Families with kids and people with dogs tend to love this about living there. You can get out of the house on foot or bike and actually go somewhere without immediately hitting traffic.

The Redmond Ridge Community Center at Cedar Park has tennis and volleyball courts, meeting space, an outdoor amphitheater, and is used for community events. The broader park system includes more than 14 parks and miles of trails maintained by the ROA. Redmond Ridge East has a QFC, some restaurants, and everyday commercial needs. It's not a walkscore neighborhood in the urban sense, you'll drive for most errands and restaurants, but the internal trail and park infrastructure is genuinely above average for a suburban development.

Who Tends to Do Well Here and Who Doesn't

Redmond Ridge works well for families who want newer construction quality, a lot of trail access, good schools, and a neighborhood with some cohesion. It also works for buyers pricing themselves out of closer in Redmond or Kirkland who still want a functional commute to the Eastside tech corridor.

It tends to disappoint buyers who want walkability to restaurants and coffee, buyers who want an older home with character and a larger lot, and anyone whose daily commute involves a lot of time on the west side of the lake. The location is genuinely east of everything. If you're doing a Seattle commute regularly, factor that drive honestly.

It also doesn't suit buyers who strongly dislike HOA living. The HOA here is active and the rules are real. That's not a criticism, it's just a relevant fact about what you're signing up for.

Pricing Context

Redmond Ridge generally prices below the comparable square footage you'd find in Kirkland or central Redmond, and below the comparable lot size you'd find in older Sammamish. That gap exists because of the location premium you give up by being further east. Whether that tradeoff makes sense depends on your priorities. If square footage, newer construction, and trail access matter more than proximity, the value here is real. If commute time and urban walkability are the priority, the discount exists for a reason.

Common Questions

Is Redmond Ridge actually in Redmond?

It uses a Redmond mailing address, but Redmond Ridge sits in unincorporated King County rather than inside Redmond city limits. For day to day life this usually doesn't matter much, but it affects permitting, some services, and occasionally property tax details. Worth knowing before you buy.

What HOA fees look like in Redmond Ridge?

HOA dues vary depending on which subneighborhood within Redmond Ridge you're buying in, so you'll want to verify the specific amount for any home you're seriously considering. The HOA covers common areas, the community center, parks, and trail maintenance, so you're generally getting something for the cost, but it's a real ongoing expense to factor in.

How does Redmond Ridge compare to the Sammamish Plateau?

They're similar in some ways: both are planned communities with good schools, newer construction, and trail access. Sammamish Plateau tends to have more variety in lot size and older stock mixed in from the 1990s. Redmond Ridge has a more cohesive community feel with its own center and commercial node. Sammamish is generally a longer drive from the Microsoft and Overlake corridors than Redmond Ridge, though both require some commute planning.

What is the community center like and is it worth using?

The Redmond Ridge Community Center at Cedar Park has tennis and volleyball courts, meeting rooms, a large fireplace, an outdoor amphitheater, and space for community events. The broader ROA park and trail system is what most residents get the most day to day use out of. It's genuinely used and maintained, not one of those facilities that's nice on paper and empty in practice.

Are there resale concerns with master planned communities that buyers should think about?

The main thing to think about is that because Redmond Ridge was built out in a concentrated period, a lot of homes hit certain age related maintenance thresholds around the same time, roofs, exterior paint, appliances. When you're buying a home here, look carefully at what has been updated and what hasn't, because deferred maintenance on 2000s construction isn't always obvious from a first walkthrough. A good inspection matters here just as much as anywhere else.

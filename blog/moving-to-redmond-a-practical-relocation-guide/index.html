Moving to Redmond WA: What You Actually Need to Know

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

People ask me about Redmond a lot, and usually they already know the basic pitch: tech jobs, good schools, nice neighborhoods. What they actually want to know is what it's like to live there once you get past that. So let me try to give you the more useful version.

Why People End Up in Redmond

The honest answer for most people relocating here is Microsoft. The main campus sits right in Redmond, and over the past several years Amazon, Google, Meta, and a handful of other tech companies have expanded their footprints nearby enough that Redmond ends up on a lot of relocation shortlists. If you're commuting to the Eastside tech corridor, Redmond just makes geographic sense.

That said, plenty of people move here with no tech connection at all. The schools pull families from outside the area, and the housing stock is varied enough that you're not locked into one type of home or one price point. Redmond is also one of the few Eastside cities where you can still find a yard, a reasonable lot size, and a neighborhood that doesn't feel like it was built entirely in one decade.

The Neighborhoods Are Not Interchangeable

This is the thing that trips up a lot of buyers who are relocating from out of state and doing their research online. Redmond is not one uniform place. The experience of living in Education Hill versus Redmond Ridge versus downtown Redmond versus Bear Creek is pretty different. Here's a quick breakdown of how they actually differ.

Education Hill

This is the central residential area most families gravitate toward, and it deserves its reputation. The streets are established, the lots are real sized, and you're close to Redmond Town Center, the library, and the parks system. Most of the housing here was built in the 1970s to 1990s, so you'll find a mix of older ranches, split levels, and larger colonials that have been updated to varying degrees. It's the kind of neighborhood where people stay a long time, which also means inventory doesn't turn over constantly.

Redmond Ridge

This is the newer, more planned part of the city, out toward the eastern edge. The homes are mostly 2000s and 2010s construction, so you get more consistent finishes and less deferred maintenance to worry about. The tradeoff is that you're farther from downtown Redmond and from the main employment centers, so commutes add up. The community itself is well maintained with trails and parks built into the development, but it has more of a planned suburb feel than Education Hill does.

Overlake and the Area Near Microsoft Campus

If you're working at Microsoft and want to minimize your commute to something you can actually walk or bike, the neighborhoods immediately around campus are worth looking at seriously. The housing mix here is more eclectic. You'll find older homes, townhomes, and some condo options. It's not as residential feeling as Education Hill, but the convenience is real, and you're close to Bellevue as well via SR 520 or surface streets.

Downtown Redmond

The downtown core has changed a lot in the last ten years. Both the Redmond Technology Station and the Downtown Redmond Station on the 2 Line are now open, and together they're reshaping what's being built here. There's more density, more townhomes and condos, more walkable amenities. If you want the ability to walk to dinner or coffee without getting in a car, downtown is your best option in Redmond. If you want a yard and quiet streets, it's probably not the right fit.

Schools: The Short Version

Most of Redmond falls within Lake Washington School District, which consistently ranks among the stronger public school districts in Washington state. The specifics matter though. Schools vary, and your assigned school depends on where exactly you buy, not just which city. If a particular school is a deciding factor for your family, verify the boundary before you make an offer, not after. The district's boundary tool is easy enough to use, and I'd rather you spend fifteen minutes confirming that than be surprised later.

Redmond High School is the main comprehensive public high school with its campus in Redmond, and it has a strong academic program. The district has several comprehensive high schools in total, and depending on exactly where you buy, your assigned high school may differ, so always confirm with the district's school locator. The middle school and elementary situation is worth looking at too if you have younger kids.

Getting Around: Commute Realities

Redmond is not a city where you can fully escape your car yet, though it's getting closer depending on where you live and where you're going. Here's what actually matters.

If you're heading to the Microsoft main campus, you can be there in minutes from most of Education Hill. That's a genuine advantage. If you're commuting to Seattle, the SR 520 bridge is your main route, and it has tolls that vary by time of day. The 2 Line light rail now connects Redmond all the way through to Seattle, which is a bigger deal than it might sound if you've been sitting in 520 traffic for years.

Heading south to Bellevue is easy on most days. I 405 is accessible, though like anywhere on the Eastside, there are stretches of rush hour where things back up. Kirkland and Woodinville are both close if you end up with business or family pulling you those directions regularly.

If you're relocating from a major city with robust transit, be realistic about the fact that you will probably still need a car here, at least for now. The network is improving, but Redmond is still a driving city for most residents.

What the Housing Market Is Actually Like

Redmond sits at a price point above Kirkland in many cases, mostly because of the tech employment base nearby driving sustained demand. Single family homes in Education Hill typically trade in a range that reflects that demand, and competition can be real on well priced listings, especially in spring. That said, it's not as extreme as some pockets of the Eastside where things spiral into bidding wars on every half decent listing.

Townhomes and attached homes have become more common, particularly around downtown and the Overlake area, and they represent a lower entry point for buyers who want Redmond but can't quite stretch to the single family price range right now.

One thing worth knowing if you're relocating: Redmond's inventory has historically been tight. This is a city where people buy and stay, which is a good sign for resale down the road, but it means you may not have a large pool of homes to choose from at any given moment. Having your financing sorted out before you start touring seriously isn't just good advice here, it's almost mandatory if you want to move quickly when the right place comes up.

Outdoor Life and What You'll Actually Do Weekends

This is an underrated part of the Redmond picture. The Sammamish River Trail runs through the city and connects down to Marymoor Park, which is a legitimate destination. Marymoor has a climbing wall, a velodrome, a large off leash dog area, and regularly hosts outdoor concerts. It's the kind of park that makes you feel like you live somewhere that thought about quality of life, not just density.

Bear Creek and Evans Creek Preserve offer more natural, quieter hiking if Marymoor feels too social. And you're close enough to the Cascades that weekend ski trips to Snoqualmie Pass or longer drives to Stevens are realistic on a regular basis.

Redmond Town Center is the main retail and restaurant hub and handles most of the basic errands and dining you'll need. It's not a dense urban walkable district, but it works well for what it is.

A Few Things People Don't Think to Ask About

Rain. This is obvious if you've done any research on the Pacific Northwest, but Redmond specifically sits in an area that gets meaningful precipitation. The gray stretches in winter are real, and if you're coming from a sunnier climate, it's worth going in with open eyes. Most people adjust, but some don't. Worth knowing yourself before you commit to a zip code.

Traffic on 148th Ave NE and around the Microsoft campus during peak hours can be genuinely bad. If you're buying near there, it's worth driving through on a Tuesday morning to see what you're signing up for, not on a weekend afternoon when nothing's moving anyway.

Redmond is not cheap. If budget is a constraint and you're flexible on location, Kirkland, Bothell, and even parts of Woodinville can offer more house for the money with similar access to schools and amenities. It depends on what you're prioritizing, but Redmond commands a premium that's real and worth factoring in honestly.

Common Questions

Is Redmond a good place to live if I don't work in tech?

Yes, though the city's culture and economy are pretty closely tied to the tech industry, so you'll notice that influence. The schools, parks, and neighborhoods are good regardless of what you do for work. Plenty of non tech families live here happily. The main consideration is whether the price point makes sense for your budget, since the tech employment base keeps demand and prices elevated.

How does Redmond compare to Kirkland for a family relocating to the Eastside?

They're close in a lot of ways but feel different day to day. Kirkland has more of a waterfront identity and a denser downtown core with more walkable options near the lake. Redmond tends to have a quieter, more neighborhood feel outside of the downtown area, and the lots in Education Hill run a bit larger on average. Kirkland can be slightly more affordable depending on the specific area and home type. If proximity to Microsoft campus is a priority, Redmond has a clear edge. If you want to walk to coffee and the waterfront on weekends, Kirkland probably wins.

What should I know about the light rail situation in Redmond?

Both the Redmond Technology Station and the Downtown Redmond Station are now open on the 2 Line, and as of 2026 the full line connects through to Seattle. For people who commute to Seattle, this is a genuine option worth looking into, especially if you live near one of the stations. It won't eliminate car dependency for most people, but it's a real alternative for the Seattle commute specifically.

Are there condos or lower entry price points in Redmond, or is it all single family?

There are condos and townhomes, mostly concentrated around downtown Redmond and the Overlake area. The entry points there are lower than single family homes in Education Hill, though Redmond is still not cheap by any measure. If budget is a real constraint, it's worth comparing what you can get in Redmond in an attached home versus a single family home in a nearby city like Bothell or Woodinville.

How competitive is the housing market in Redmond right now?

Inventory is generally tight in Redmond because turnover is low. Well priced homes in good condition in Education Hill can move quickly and sometimes see multiple offers, particularly in spring. The market isn't uniformly frenzied, but you shouldn't assume you'll have weeks to decide. Getting preapproval lined up before you start seriously touring is important here.

How to Price Your Home Right on the Eastside

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

The number you pick on day one matters more than anything else you do

Sellers spend weeks on paint colors, staging, and landscaping. That stuff matters. But the single biggest lever you have as a seller is the price you put on the home when it hits the market. Get that right and everything else gets easier. Get it wrong and you are fighting uphill from the moment you go live.

Pricing on the Eastside is not a simple exercise. You are dealing with a market where Bellevue, Kirkland, Redmond, Sammamish, Issaquah, Woodinville, and Bothell all behave differently, sometimes block by block. A price that would be aggressive in one zip code might be conservative in a neighborhood two miles away. That granularity is where the real work happens.

What a real pricing analysis actually looks at

Most sellers have heard of comparables, the recent sold homes that form the foundation of any pricing conversation. But pulling comps is not just grabbing the three highest sales in your zip code and averaging them. The honest version is more selective and more demanding.

The comps that matter most are homes that a buyer would genuinely have considered alongside yours. Same general location, similar square footage, similar lot size, similar condition and finish level, and sold recently enough that the data is still relevant. On the Eastside right now, I give the most weight to sales from the last 60 to 90 days. Anything older than that starts to reflect a different market moment.

Within that pool of comps you have to make adjustments. A home with a three car garage is worth more than one with a two car garage on the same street. A south facing backyard matters in some neighborhoods. A busy arterial behind the fence matters almost everywhere. These are not big abstract numbers. They are specific line items that you can defend in a negotiation.

Active listings also belong in the conversation. They tell you what your competition looks like to buyers right now, not six months ago. If there are three similar homes sitting unsold at a price point, that is a signal worth paying attention to before you decide where to list.

Why the Eastside is not one market

One thing that trips sellers up is treating the Eastside as a single uniform place. It is not. Bellevue has consistently strong buyer demand and relatively tight supply, particularly in neighborhoods close to downtown. Kirkland Waterfront carries a premium that nonwaterfront Kirkland does not. Sammamish tends to attract families who want newer construction and larger lots and are willing to accept the driving that comes with it. Woodinville and Bothell offer more value per square foot but serve a buyer pool that is often more price sensitive than Bellevue buyers.

What this means practically is that a pricing strategy that works in one of these cities can fall flat in another. The psychology of the buyer shifts, the pool of competing homes shifts, and the acceptable price per square foot shifts. You have to know which version of the Eastside market you are actually in.

The case against overpricing

Almost every seller I talk to initially wants to list high and see what happens. The logic feels reasonable. You can always come down. Leaving money on the table is worse than trying. I understand the instinct, but the data on this is pretty consistent and not in favor of that strategy.

Homes on the Eastside typically get the most buyer attention in the first seven to ten days on market. That is when the buyers who have been actively searching and are genuinely ready to move jump on a new listing. If the price is off, those buyers skip it, or they come, feel like it is overpriced, and leave without writing an offer. Once a listing sits past that first week or two, something changes in how buyers perceive it. They start to wonder what is wrong with it. The days on market number follows the listing everywhere, and buyers and their agents notice it.

Price reductions attract less attention than new listings do. By the time you cut the price to where it should have started, some of the best buyers have moved on to other homes. You often end up selling for less than you would have if you had priced correctly from the beginning.

I have seen this play out enough times that I am direct about it with sellers even when the conversation is uncomfortable. The goal is the best net result, not the most optimistic starting number.

When pricing below market value makes sense

There are situations where deliberately pricing below recent comps is the right call. In a competitive seller's market with limited inventory and motivated buyers, a sharp price can generate enough competing offers that the home sells well above where it started. This is not a trick. It only works when the conditions genuinely support it, meaning enough buyers are actively looking, inventory is low enough that your home stands out, and the home itself is prepared and presented well enough to create urgency.

It is a strategy, and like any strategy it requires honest assessment of current conditions rather than wishful thinking. When the market is softer and buyer traffic is lighter, this approach can backfire. You price low hoping for multiple offers and get one offer at the low price with no competition to push it up.

What condition and presentation actually do to price

Pricing is not just about the comps. It also has to account honestly for the condition of your home relative to what sold. If the best comparable sale was a fully updated kitchen with quartz counters and new appliances, and your kitchen has not been touched since 2003, those homes are not equivalent. Buyers will make that adjustment whether or not you do.

This is one reason I think getting the home right before listing is worth serious attention. A home that shows well, photographs well, and has addressed obvious deferred maintenance will defend its price better than one that is priced the same but gives buyers reasons to discount. Buyers and their agents are looking for any reason to justify a lower offer. You want to give them fewer of those reasons, not more.

How interest rates affect where buyers draw the line

Rates matter to pricing in a way that is sometimes underappreciated. When rates move, buyer purchasing power moves with them. A buyer who could comfortably afford a $1.4 million home when rates were at one level may be squeezed at a higher rate environment, not because their income changed but because the monthly payment did.

On the Eastside, where prices are high enough that most buyers are carrying substantial loans, this effect is real. Certain price thresholds become psychological breaking points for buyers. Pricing your home just above one of those thresholds without strong justification can reduce your buyer pool more than you expect.

This is worth factoring into your pricing conversation rather than treating as background noise.

What to do if you and your agent disagree on price

This happens. Sellers have an emotional and financial stake in their home that is completely legitimate, and it is natural to want a number that reflects everything you have put into the property. Sometimes the market supports that number and sometimes it does not.

My approach is to show the work. I will walk through every comparable sale, explain the adjustments I made, show you the active competition, and tell you exactly where I think the price lands and why. If you disagree with my conclusion, I want to know which part of the analysis you see differently. That is a real conversation. What does not help either of us is picking a number because it feels right or because a neighbor said their house sold for X.

If after that conversation we are still far apart on price, that is important information. It might mean we are not the right fit for each other, and that is okay. Working with a seller who is committed to a price the market will not support is a bad outcome for both sides.

A few things sellers often underestimate

  • The difference in buyer traffic between a home priced at $1,395,000 and one priced at $1,450,000 can be significant even if the gap feels small to the seller.
  • Seasonal timing affects pricing too. What a home can realistically achieve in March is often different from what it can achieve in November, even if the underlying market has not changed much.
  • Buyer agents talk to each other. If your home gets a reputation as overpriced, that perception spreads faster than you might think.
  • A price that generates no showings in the first ten days is sending you a clear signal. Waiting longer rarely changes the outcome.

Getting the price right is ultimately about being honest with yourself and the market about what the data says. The Eastside is a strong market and a good time to sell is relative, but none of that changes the basic reality that buyers have choices and they are making comparisons. A home priced well for what it is will find its buyer. A home priced for what you wish it were worth is a harder road.

Common Questions

How do I know if a comp my agent used is actually comparable to my home?

Ask your agent to walk you through why each sale was included. The useful questions are: Would a buyer who looked at that home also look at mine? Are the lot sizes and locations genuinely similar? Is the finish level and condition close enough that the numbers are meaningful? If a comp is being used because it sold nearby at a high price but the homes are quite different, that is worth pushing back on.

Does it matter which month I list on the Eastside?

Yes, meaningfully. Spring, roughly late February through May, tends to produce the most buyer activity on the Eastside. Fall has a secondary window, with buyer activity picking back up after Labor Day through October. The summer slowdown is real, and listing in late November or December usually means a smaller buyer pool even if your home is priced well. That does not mean other times of year never work, but timing is a real variable in your pricing and strategy conversation.

If I get a low offer early, does that mean my price is wrong?

Not necessarily. Some buyers lowball regardless of price as a negotiating tactic. The more useful signal is whether you are getting showings and whether agents who showed the home are giving you feedback that the price feels off. One low offer from one buyer is noise. No offers and no serious traffic after two weeks is data.

How much does lot size actually affect price on the Eastside?

It depends heavily on the city and neighborhood. In parts of Bellevue and Kirkland where land is scarce, lot size and the potential to build or expand can carry significant weight. In Sammamish, lot sizes vary considerably across neighborhoods, from compact lots in master planned communities to larger parcels on the plateau, so how much weight the market gives to lot size shifts depending on the specific area you are in. Your agent should be able to show you sold comps that illustrate how the local market is actually valuing land in your specific area.

Should I price my home based on what I need to net, or based on what the market will bear?

The market does not care what you need to net. I say that plainly because sellers sometimes anchor on a number based on what they owe, what they paid, or what they need for the next purchase. Those are real constraints but they are separate from what a buyer will pay. If there is a gap between what the market supports and what you need, it is better to know that before you list than to find out three weeks in after the home has been sitting.

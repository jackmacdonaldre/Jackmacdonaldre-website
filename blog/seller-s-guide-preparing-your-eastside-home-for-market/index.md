## What actually moves the needle when you're selling on the Eastside

Sellers ask me all the time what they should do before listing. The honest answer is: it depends on the house, but there are a few things that matter almost everywhere on the Eastside, and a few things people spend money on that don't move the needle much at all.

This isn't a checklist you can print and hand to a contractor. It's more of a framework for thinking through the decisions, because the right prep for a 1988 Sammamish colonial is different from the right prep for a 2002 Kirkland townhome or a mid century Bellevue rambler.

## Start with an honest assessment of the house

Before you call a stager or pick a paint color, walk through the house the way a buyer would. Come in through the front door. Look up at the ceiling. Open the cabinet under the kitchen sink. Flush the toilets. Turn on the faucets.

Buyers notice the things that suggest deferred maintenance more than they notice cosmetic imperfections. A small water stain on a ceiling will get written into every offer you receive, either as a price reduction request or a repair contingency. A fresh coat of paint somewhere else won't offset it.

The goal at this stage is to separate the must fix items from the nice to have items. Some things are worth addressing before you go to market. Others are better left disclosed and priced accordingly.

## The items worth fixing versus the ones worth skipping

Generally worth addressing before listing:

* Anything that will show up on an inspection and give a buyer leverage to renegotiate. Leaky faucets, failed window seals, an aging water heater that's past its expected life, electrical panel issues, wood rot on the exterior.
* Carpet that's clearly at the end of its life. Buyers can smell it and feel it underfoot. Replacement is relatively affordable and the visual change is significant.
* Paint in rooms with dated colors or visible scuffs and marks. Neutral doesn't mean white. It means nothing that a buyer will photograph and send to their friends with a raised eyebrow emoji.
* Landscaping that looks neglected. On the Eastside, where a lot of buyers are coming from tech jobs and don't have a lot of time for yard work, overgrown landscaping reads as future burden. A cleanup, fresh mulch, and some seasonal color near the entry costs a few hundred dollars and photographs well.

Generally not worth doing before listing:

* Full kitchen or bathroom remodels. The ROI on a full kitchen renovation before a sale is almost never there, and you won't choose finishes that appeal to every buyer anyway. Cleaning, decluttering, and making sure everything functions properly is usually more effective than new cabinets.
* Converting a garage or adding square footage. This is a long timeline and a significant spend, and you're unlikely to price it in before you close.
* Highly personalized upgrades. If you're thinking about replacing the counters with something you personally love, think twice. What you love and what photographs well for a broad buyer pool aren't always the same thing.

## Pricing on the Eastside is not a guessing game, but it requires real judgment

I'll be direct about this: pricing is where a lot of sellers lose money, in both directions. Overpricing is the more common mistake, but I've also seen sellers leave value on the table by pricing conservatively in a neighborhood with real demand.

On the Eastside, the price per square foot can vary significantly even within the same city. In Bellevue, a home in the West Bellevue or Medina corridor is priced against a completely different buyer pool than a home in the Newport Hills or Somerset area. In Kirkland, homes near the waterfront or in the Bridle Trails area command premiums that a home a mile east won't get, even if they're otherwise comparable.

The right comp isn't just a similar square footage in a nearby zip code. It's a home with a similar lot, similar school access, similar commute convenience, and a similar renovation level that sold within the last 60 to 90 days. When the market shifts, older comps can mislead you in either direction.

I price homes by looking at what sold, what's currently active and sitting, and what went pending quickly. The homes that sit are often as instructive as the ones that sold fast.

## Staging: when it helps and when it's theater

Staging matters most in two situations: vacant homes and homes with unconventional layouts. A vacant home feels smaller than it is and gives buyers nothing to respond to emotionally. A staged vacant home almost always photographs and shows better than an empty one.

For occupied homes, the biggest staging wins are usually editing rather than adding. Remove half of what's on the kitchen counters. Clear out the garage enough that a buyer can see it's a functional two car garage. Take personal photographs off the main living areas. Let the house be a house rather than someone's home.

Professional staging services on the Eastside vary widely in cost and quality. For most homes in the $1 million to $2 million range, a consultation with a stager followed by selective furniture rental for the main living areas and primary bedroom is usually enough. Full house staging on a larger home can run considerably more and isn't always proportionate to the return.

## Photography and how homes are presented online

Most buyers see your home for the first time on a screen. The listing photos are the first showing. This isn't a place to cut corners.

Professional photography with proper lighting makes a material difference. Wide angle lenses used honestly (not deceptively) show space well. Twilight exterior shots can be compelling for the right home. Video walkthroughs and 3D tours have become more expected, especially for homes in the $1.5 million and above range.

One thing I'd flag: there's a difference between photography that makes a home look its best and photography that sets unrealistic expectations. If a buyer arrives and the home looks nothing like the photos, you've started that relationship with disappointment. Accurate and flattering is the goal, not flattering at the expense of accuracy.

## Timing your Eastside listing

Spring, meaning late February through May, is historically the most active period for Eastside real estate. More buyers are looking, which generally creates more competition for well priced homes. But it also means more competition from other sellers.

Fall, September through early November, is typically a solid secondary window. Buyers who didn't find something in spring are often still active, and inventory tends to drop going into winter, which can work in a seller's favor.

The slowest periods are typically mid December through January and the hottest weeks of August when families are traveling. Listing during those stretches isn't impossible, but you're fishing in a smaller pond.

That said, the right time to list also depends on your house and your situation. A home in a neighborhood with very low turnover can generate strong interest whenever it hits the market, because buyers in that area have been waiting. And sometimes personal timing matters more than market timing.

## Disclosure: don't try to hide things

Washington is a disclosure state, and sellers are required to complete a seller disclosure statement disclosing known material defects. Beyond the legal requirement, there's a practical argument for being thorough: buyers who discover something after closing that you knew about will pursue remedies, and those conversations are far more unpleasant than a price negotiation before closing.

If your home has had water intrusion, a pest issue that was treated, work done without permits, or a neighbor dispute that affects the property, disclose it. A buyer who knows about an issue upfront can make an informed decision. A buyer who finds it during inspection (or worse, after closing) feels misled, and they usually are.

Sellers sometimes worry that disclosing something will kill the deal. In my experience, transparency handled upfront is almost always less damaging than the same information surfacing mid transaction.

## Common Questions

### Should I get a presale inspection before listing my Eastside home?

It's worth considering, especially if the home is older or you haven't done a lot of maintenance tracking. A presale inspection lets you find the issues before a buyer's inspector does, so you can decide how to address them on your own terms rather than under the pressure of a pending deal. The downside is that anything found in a presale inspection generally needs to be disclosed. But in most cases, knowing what's there and getting ahead of it is better than being surprised in the middle of a transaction.

### How much should I expect to spend on preparation before listing?

It varies a lot depending on the home's condition and price point. For a well maintained home that just needs cleaning, paint touch ups, and landscaping cleanup, you might spend $5,000 to $10,000. For a home that needs carpet replacement, exterior paint, and professional staging, you could be looking at $20,000 to $40,000 or more. The question to ask is not how much you're spending but whether the expected return in a higher sale price or faster sale justifies the cost. That math is different for every house.

### Does the school district still matter for Eastside home prices even if I'm selling to buyers without kids?

Yes, and more than most sellers expect. School district assignment affects resale value broadly because even buyers without school age children today know that future buyers will care. Homes in the Lake Washington School District or Bellevue School District tend to hold and often command a premium relative to otherwise similar homes in other districts. It's worth understanding where your home falls before you price it, because that context affects who your buyer pool will be. Confirm your exact district assignment using the district's own school locator, since boundaries can run street by street.

### What's the biggest mistake Eastside sellers make when preparing to list?

Overimproving in the wrong places. I see sellers spend significant money on a kitchen renovation or a bathroom addition when the buyers in that price range would have renovated it themselves to their own taste anyway. The more reliable preparation investments are the ones that remove objections rather than add features: fix the deferred maintenance, freshen the paint, clean everything, and present the house clearly. That approach will get you further than a remodel most of the time.

### Is it worth waiting for spring if my home is ready now?

Maybe, but not always. If you're ready in January, waiting until March buys you a larger buyer pool but also more competing listings. If your home has something specific that makes it appealing year round, like a great floor plan, a coveted school zone, or a location near major employers in Bellevue or Redmond, it may not need the seasonal lift as much. The spring advantage is real but not universal, and sitting on a ready home for two or three months carries its own costs.

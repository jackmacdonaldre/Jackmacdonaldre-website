# Selling a Rental Property With Tenants in Bellevue WA

> Selling a Bellevue rental with tenants in place? Here is what Washington landlords need to know about notice, leases, and timing before listing.

## This comes up more than you'd think

A fair number of the sellers I work with in Bellevue aren't selling a home they've been living in. They're selling a property that has tenants in it, sometimes a long term renter who's been there for years, sometimes a situation that's gotten complicated. Either way, the process is meaningfully different from a typical sale, and there are real legal constraints in Washington that trip people up if they don't know what they're dealing with ahead of time.

This isn't legal advice and you should absolutely talk to a real estate attorney before making major moves here. But I can walk you through the practical landscape so you know what questions to ask and what decisions actually matter.

## Your lease is the first document to look at

Washington is a state where tenant rights are taken seriously, and that starts with the lease. If your tenant has a fixed term lease, say they're in month four of a twelve month agreement, you generally cannot force them to leave just because you've decided to sell. The lease travels with the property, and a buyer who purchases the home takes it subject to that lease. That changes your buyer pool significantly. Some buyers will walk away, some investors will be fine with it, and others will want a discount to account for the transition period.

Month to month tenancies give you more flexibility, but Washington law still requires proper written notice. As of recent changes to state law, landlords must provide a reason (a legal "just cause") to end a month to month tenancy in many situations, and the required notice period depends on the specific cause. For the sale of a single family residence, state law requires at least 90 days advance written notice to the tenant. That is not a small window when you're trying to time a sale.

## Washington's Just Cause Eviction Law and What It Means for Sellers

Washington passed significant tenant protections in recent years. The "just cause" eviction law means you generally can't terminate a month to month tenancy simply because you want to sell. However, under RCW 59.18.650(2)(e), when an owner elects to sell a single family residence, that is a recognized just cause to end the tenancy. The requirement is at least 90 days advance written notice, and you must actually follow through on listing and marketing the property. The notice itself must identify the specific facts and circumstances supporting the cause, and errors in the notice can reset the clock and delay everything.

If your plan is to sell to another investor who will keep the tenant in place, this whole issue is sidestepped. The tenant stays, the lease stays, the new owner steps into your shoes. That can actually be a cleaner transaction in some cases.

## The Practical Problem: Showings

Even if you have every legal right to sell and show the property, tenants can make the process difficult. Washington law requires landlords to give at least one day's written notice before entering a rental unit to show it to prospective purchasers. Some tenants are cooperative. Others are not, and they don't have to be. I've seen situations where a tenant technically complied with access rules but kept the place in rough shape, declined to leave during open houses, or made comments to buyers that hurt the sale.

None of that is illegal on the tenant's part, and there's not much you can do about it other than try to build a good working relationship before you list. If you have a strained relationship with your tenant already, that's worth factoring into your strategy before you do anything else.

## Cash for Keys: Often Worth Considering

One approach that works reasonably well, when the tenant is open to it, is offering cash for keys. This is essentially a negotiated agreement where the tenant agrees to vacate by a certain date in exchange for a payment from you. It's not guaranteed to work, and tenants have no obligation to accept, but it can be significantly faster and cheaper than navigating the formal notice process, especially if you're looking at a 90 day notice period under state law.

What's a reasonable amount? It depends on how long they've been there, what market rents look like, and how motivated they are. There's no formula, but framing it as help with their moving costs and first month's rent somewhere new tends to land better than framing it as "please leave so I can sell."

## Pricing and Buyer Pool Considerations

If you're selling with a tenant in place, particularly under a fixed term lease, your likely buyers are investors. In Bellevue and the broader Eastside market, there is investor demand, but it's more selective than in some other markets. Investors here want to understand the rent relative to current market rates, the condition of the property, and what the lease terms look like. If the tenant is paying below market rent under a long lease, that affects what an investor will pay.

Pricing a tenanted property is different from pricing an owner occupied home. The income the property generates matters. So does the lease expiration date. A buyer who wants to eventually move in themselves will weight a near term lease expiration very differently than one with two years left.

It's also worth being honest about condition. When a tenant has been in a property for several years, there's often deferred maintenance you haven't been able to get in and address. What you can't inspect, you can't fix. That tends to show up in buyer offers.

## Timing the Sale Around the Tenancy

Sometimes the cleanest path is to wait. If your tenant's lease ends in four months and you're in a reasonable position to wait, listing after they've moved out lets you prep the home properly, photograph it empty or staged, and sell to the full range of buyers including owner occupants who represent the most competition and typically the strongest offers in Bellevue's residential market.

That said, waiting has carrying costs: mortgage, taxes, insurance, and utilities if the unit is vacant. And the market doesn't stay the same. It's a real tradeoff and there's no universal right answer, but it's worth running the numbers on what a few months of holding costs actually looks like versus what you might net from a broader buyer pool later.

## What to Do Before You List

A few things worth getting sorted before you put the property on the market:

* Pull your lease and read it carefully. Know the expiration date, any renewal clauses, and what it says about access and showing rights.

* Talk to a Washington landlord tenant attorney, especially if you need to serve any kind of notice. The state laws here have been updated significantly in recent years and the details matter.

* Have a conversation with your tenant before the for sale sign goes up. How they find out matters. Tenants who feel blindsided are rarely cooperative.

* Decide early whether you're targeting investors, owner occupants, or both, because that decision shapes pricing, timing, and how you handle the tenancy.

Selling a rental property is doable. It just requires more planning than a typical sale, and the legal side of it in Washington deserves real attention before you start the clock on anything.

## Common Questions

### Can I sell my Bellevue rental property while a tenant is still living there?

Yes, you can sell with a tenant in place. If the tenant has a fixed term lease, the buyer typically takes the property subject to that lease. If the tenancy is month to month, Washington's just cause eviction law governs whether and how you can end it before selling. For a single family residence, the owner electing to sell is a recognized just cause under RCW 59.18.650(2)(e), and state law requires at least 90 days advance written notice to the tenant.

### Does Washington State require just cause to end a month to month tenancy?

Yes, in most cases. Washington's just cause eviction law requires a specific legal reason to end most residential tenancies. When an owner elects to sell a single family residence, that is a recognized just cause under RCW 59.18.650(2)(e), but you must give the tenant at least 90 days advance written notice, the notice must state specific facts supporting the cause, and you must follow through on listing and marketing the property. Talk to an attorney before serving any notice.

### How much notice do I have to give a tenant before showing the property?

Under RCW 59.18.150, Washington state law requires landlords to give at least one day's written notice before entering a rental unit to show it to prospective purchasers or tenants. The general notice period for other types of entry, such as inspections or repairs, is at least two days. You can arrange more frequent showings with the tenant's consent, but you cannot force access on shorter notice. A cooperative tenant makes this process much smoother.

### Will having a tenant hurt my sale price?

It can, depending on the situation. If the tenant is paying below market rent under a long lease, an investor buyer will factor that into their offer. If the property is in rough condition because access for maintenance has been limited, that shows up too. Owner occupant buyers largely won't compete for a home with a tenant in a fixed term lease, which shrinks your pool. Sometimes waiting until the lease ends and selling vacant nets you more, sometimes the holding costs make that not worth it.

### What is cash for keys and is it legal in Washington?

Cash for keys is an informal agreement where a landlord offers the tenant money in exchange for vacating by a specific date. It's legal and commonly used. The tenant has no obligation to accept, and it works best when you have a decent relationship and the tenant is already thinking about moving. It can save significant time compared to waiting out a formal notice period.

### What type of buyer should I expect if my property has a long term tenant in place?

Mostly investors. Owner occupants who want to live in the home generally won't buy a property they can't take possession of for a year or more. Investors on the Eastside do buy tenanted properties, but they'll underwrite the deal based on the lease terms, current rent versus market rent, and property condition. If you want the full buyer pool including owner occupants, the cleaner path is usually waiting until the unit is vacant.

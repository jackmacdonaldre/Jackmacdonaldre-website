What Happens After Your Offer Is Accepted in Washington State

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

Most buyers spend weeks or months preparing to write an offer. Then it gets accepted, and suddenly nobody told them what happens next. The period between accepted offer and closing is about 30 to 45 days on average for a financed purchase, and a lot goes on during that window. Missing a deadline or misunderstanding a step can cost you money, your earnest money, or the deal itself.

Here is what that process actually looks like in Washington State, in the order things tend to happen.

Earnest Money Goes Out Fast

In Washington, earnest money is typically due within two business days of mutual acceptance under the standard NWMLS purchase and sale agreement. That is the moment both parties have signed and the contract is fully executed. Your agent should confirm the exact deadline, because it is written into the purchase and sale agreement and missing it is a serious problem.

The money goes into escrow, usually held by a title company. It is not sent to the seller. It sits in a neutral account until closing, at which point it gets applied toward your down payment or closing costs. If the deal closes normally, you never really think about it again.

What buyers sometimes do not realize: the amount of earnest money you offered matters. In competitive situations around Bellevue and the Eastside, one to three percent of the purchase price is common. A $1.5 million home might have $30,000 or more sitting in escrow. That is real money at risk if you back out without a valid contingency.

Opening Escrow and the Title Order

Your agent or the listing agent will open escrow with a title company shortly after mutual acceptance. The title company orders a preliminary title report on the property. This document shows any liens, easements, or encumbrances attached to the home, things like unpaid property taxes, an old contractor lien, or a utility easement across the backyard.

Most of the time the preliminary title report comes back clean and you barely notice it. Occasionally it surfaces something that needs to be resolved before closing. Your agent should walk you through it when it arrives so you are not reading it cold.

The Inspection Window

If your offer included an inspection contingency, you have a set number of days to conduct your inspections and decide what to do with what you find. Under the standard NWMLS inspection addendum (Form 35), the default inspection period is ten days, though buyers and sellers often negotiate a shorter window depending on the market.

A general home inspection covers the structure, roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and other major systems. Depending on the property, you might also want a sewer scope, a pest inspection, or a radon test. Older homes in certain parts of Bellevue, particularly those built in the 1960s to 1970s, may warrant extra attention to things like the electrical panel, original plumbing, or chimney condition.

After inspections, you have a few options. You can proceed as is, ask the seller to make repairs, ask for a price reduction or credit in lieu of repairs, or in some cases back out entirely and get your earnest money returned. What you can ask for and how the seller responds is a negotiation. It is also one of the more emotionally charged parts of the transaction, so going in with realistic expectations helps.

One thing worth knowing: sellers in Washington are not required to fix anything after a home inspection. They can say no. At that point you decide whether to accept the home as is or walk away.

Financing Moves in Parallel

While inspections are happening, your lender is working through the loan. You should have been preapproved before writing the offer, but preapproval is not the same as a loan commitment. After mutual acceptance, your lender will order an appraisal, verify your income and assets again, and submit the file to underwriting.

The appraisal is typically scheduled within the first week to ten days. The appraiser visits the property and determines whether it is worth at least what you are paying. If the appraisal comes in low, you have some decisions to make, pay the difference in cash, renegotiate with the seller, or potentially back out if your contract includes an appraisal contingency.

Underwriting can ask for additional documentation at any point. This is normal. Respond to your lender quickly when they ask for something. Delays in getting them what they need are one of the most common reasons closings get pushed back.

At some point before closing, usually about three business days out, your lender will send you a Closing Disclosure. This document lays out the final loan terms, your monthly payment, and what you owe at closing. Compare it carefully to the Loan Estimate you received earlier in the process and ask questions if anything looks different.

Homeowner's Insurance

Your lender will require proof of homeowner's insurance before they fund the loan. Do not wait until the week before closing to shop for this. Some properties, particularly older ones or those with certain claim histories, can be harder to insure or more expensive than you expect. Get at least two or three quotes and have your policy bound and ready to send your lender at least a week before closing.

The Final Walkthrough

You are entitled to a final walkthrough of the property, typically within the last day or two before closing. This is not a second inspection. The purpose is to confirm that the home is in the same condition it was when you wrote the offer, that the sellers have moved out if that was the agreed timeline, and that any repairs they agreed to make were actually completed.

People sometimes skip the walkthrough because they feel awkward or assume everything is fine. I would not. It takes thirty minutes and it protects you. If something has changed, you want to know before you sign closing documents, not after.

Signing and Funding

Washington is an escrow state, which means you sign closing documents at the title company, not at a table with the seller across from you. In many transactions, buyers and sellers sign separately and never see each other at closing at all.

You will sign a significant amount of paperwork. Bring a valid photo ID. If you are bringing funds to closing, the title company will give you wire instructions in advance. Wire the money early, ideally the day before or the morning of closing, and confirm with the title company that it was received. Wire fraud is a real risk in real estate transactions. Always verify wire instructions directly with the title company by phone before sending anything, using a number you looked up yourself, not one from an email.

In Washington, your signing appointment typically takes place one to two days before the actual closing date. After you sign, the lender authorizes funding. The title company then records the deed with King County (or whichever county applies) once funding is confirmed. Recording is when ownership officially transfers to you, and it typically happens the same day funding is received or the following business day.

Keys are typically released after recording is confirmed. That is when you can call it yours.

A Few Things That Catch Buyers Off Guard

  • Closing costs are real and they add up. Budget two to three percent of the purchase price in addition to your down payment as a working figure, though the total can be higher depending on your loan type and lender fees. The Closing Disclosure will show you the exact number before you get to the table.
  • You cannot make large deposits, take on new debt, or change jobs during this period without potentially affecting your loan. Talk to your lender before doing anything that touches your finances.
  • Dates in the contract matter. If your inspection contingency expires on a Friday, you need to have submitted your inspection response before then, not over the weekend.
  • Communication gaps are where deals go sideways. If you have not heard from your agent, your lender, or the title company in a few days, ask what is happening. Things can stall quietly.

The period from accepted offer to closing feels long when you are in it and then suddenly very short when deadlines start stacking up. Knowing what is coming and staying ahead of each step makes the difference between a smooth closing and a stressful one.

Common Questions

How long does it typically take to close on a home in Washington State?

Most financed transactions close in 30 to 45 days from mutual acceptance, though cash deals can close faster and transactions with complicated financing or title issues sometimes take longer. The timeline is set by the closing date in your purchase and sale agreement, so it is worth making sure that date is realistic given your lender's current workload before you finalize the contract.

What is mutual acceptance and why does it matter?

Mutual acceptance is the moment both the buyer and seller have signed the purchase and sale agreement and all parties have been notified. It is the official start of the transaction. Most deadlines in the contract, including when earnest money is due and when your inspection period begins, are calculated from this date. Your agent should confirm the mutual acceptance date and time in writing as soon as it happens.

Can I back out after my offer is accepted and get my earnest money back?

It depends on which contingencies are in your contract and whether you are still within those windows. If you back out during your inspection period using an inspection contingency, you generally get your earnest money returned. Same applies if your financing falls through and you have a financing contingency. If you back out without a valid contingency or after your contingencies have been removed, you are likely forfeiting that earnest money to the seller.

What should I bring to the closing appointment?

Bring a valid government issued photo ID. If you owe funds at closing, those should already be wired ahead of time, so confirm with your title company before the appointment that your wire was received. The signing itself typically takes 30 to 60 minutes depending on the loan type. You will not need to bring a checkbook or cash.

What happens if the appraisal comes in below the purchase price?

If your contract includes an appraisal contingency and the home appraises low, you have options. You can ask the seller to lower the price to match the appraised value. You can agree to pay the difference out of pocket between the appraised value and the purchase price. Or if the seller will not budge and you do not want to cover the gap, you can back out and get your earnest money returned. In competitive markets like Bellevue, buyers sometimes waive the appraisal contingency, which means they are on the hook for any gap. That is a significant financial risk and worth understanding clearly before you waive it.

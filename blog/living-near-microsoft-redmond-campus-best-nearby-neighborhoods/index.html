Best Neighborhoods Near Microsoft Redmond Campus

By Jack Macdonald
Macdonald Group of Compass

One of the first questions I get from people relocating for a Microsoft role is some version of: where should I actually live? And it's a reasonable question, because the answer isn't just about proximity. The campus sits in a part of the Eastside where you have a lot of options within a ten to fifteen minute drive, and those options feel pretty different from each other depending on what you're prioritizing.

Let me walk through the neighborhoods I'd actually point someone toward, and what matters about each one.

Redmond itself: closest, but it comes with tradeoffs

The obvious starting point is Redmond. If you're on the Redmond Town Center side of campus, parts of central Redmond are genuinely close, sometimes walkable or bikeable depending on where exactly you land at the company. The Sammamish River Trail runs through the area, and Microsoft's main campus is adjacent to it via Marymoor Park, so if you're the type who wants to commute by bike a few days a week, Redmond makes that possible in a way most Eastside cities don't.

Housing in Redmond runs the range. Older neighborhoods like Education Hill have more established lots, mature trees, and a mix of 1970s to 1990s construction that can feel more spacious than newer stuff. Closer to downtown Redmond and along Bear Creek you'll find newer townhomes and condos. The tradeoff: Redmond's inventory is smaller than Bellevue or Kirkland, so when something good comes up, it moves fast.

Schools in the Lake Washington School District, which covers much of Redmond, are consistently strong. That matters to a lot of people relocating with kids, and it's one reason demand here stays steady.

Kirkland: the lifestyle pick

Kirkland comes up constantly with Microsoft employees, and I think it's because it offers something the other options don't: actual waterfront access and a downtown that feels like a real place to spend time. The drive from most of Kirkland to the Microsoft campus is in the fifteen to twenty minute range depending on where you're coming from and what time you leave, which is very manageable.

Juanita and North Kirkland neighborhoods put you a bit closer to Redmond, and they tend to have larger lots and older home stock compared to the townhome heavy sections near downtown. South Kirkland is closer to I 405 and SR 520, which is useful if you're ever commuting toward Seattle or Bellevue.

The tradeoff with Kirkland is price. Waterfront and near waterfront properties carry a premium that gets steep quickly, and even nonwaterfront parts of Kirkland have appreciated significantly. You're also paying for the lifestyle, which some people genuinely use and others realize they don't as much as they thought.

Sammamish: space and schools, less character

Sammamish is worth considering if your priorities are square footage, newer construction, and school quality, and you're willing to accept that it's largely suburban in feel. The housing stock here skews newer than most Eastside cities, with a lot built in the 2000s to 2010s. Lots are often larger than what you'd find in Bellevue or south Kirkland at a similar price point.

The commute to Microsoft's Redmond campus from most of Sammamish runs twenty to thirty minutes depending on exactly where you are and traffic conditions on SR 202 (also known as Redmond Fall City Road) or other plateau arterials. It's not bad, but it's also not short. The bigger issue is that there's essentially one way in and one way out for a lot of Sammamish residents, and when something happens on those corridors, everyone knows it.

Sammamish is served by three separate school districts: Lake Washington School District covers roughly the northern half, Issaquah School District covers much of the rest, and a smaller portion falls under Snoqualmie Valley School District. It's worth confirming which district a specific address falls under before you get too far along.

Bellevue: the biggest city, the most range

Bellevue is a reasonable option, particularly if you have a partner commuting to Seattle or if you want the most urban environment on the Eastside. The commute to Microsoft's Redmond campus from central Bellevue is typically twenty to thirty minutes, though Sound Transit's 2 Line, which now connects Redmond to Bellevue and Seattle, changes the calculus a bit for people open to transit.

What Bellevue has that the others don't is sheer variety. You can find dense walkable areas near downtown, quieter residential neighborhoods in the Somerset or Factoria areas, and everything in between. The school district is well regarded. The amenities are there. It's also the most expensive market on the Eastside, and the range is wide enough that generalities don't serve you well here. Northeast Bellevue is quite different from West Bellevue in both price and feel.

Woodinville: if you want land and don't mind the commute

Woodinville comes up less often in Microsoft relocation conversations, but it's worth mentioning for a specific type of buyer. If you want more acreage, a quieter setting, and you like the idea of being near wine country (this area has a legitimate concentration of tasting rooms, well over a hundred by most counts), Woodinville delivers something the more suburban Eastside cities don't.

The commute to Redmond is real, though. Depending on where in Woodinville you live and what time you leave, plan on anywhere from twenty to forty five minutes, and it can be worse during peak hours. If you're hybrid or doing a few days a week in office, it's workable. As a daily commute, it's a bigger commitment.

What actually drives the decision for most people

In my experience, the people who land somewhere they're really happy with have usually made a deliberate choice about one thing they're not willing to compromise on, and then been flexible on the rest. Someone who's firm on a bike commute ends up in Redmond. Someone who wants their kids in a specific school makes the district the filter and works backward from there. Someone who values walkable evenings with their family lands in Kirkland or downtown Redmond.

The people who struggle are usually trying to optimize everything at once: short commute, big lot, great schools, urban walkability, and reasonable price. That combination doesn't exist on the Eastside right now, and you'll spend a long time looking if you hold out for it.

Worth knowing: Microsoft's campus in Redmond has expanded and continues to evolve. The parts of campus near NE 36th Street and 156th Avenue NE have different internal commute patterns than the older core, so if you know your building assignment, factor that in rather than just routing to a generic campus address.

A note on the light rail picture

Sound Transit's 2 Line, the East Link extension, now runs the full route connecting Downtown Redmond to Bellevue and Seattle via the I 90 floating bridge. The line opened in stages, with service to Redmond Technology Station (adjacent to the Microsoft campus) beginning in April 2024, an extension to Downtown Redmond opening in May 2025, and the final crosslake connection to Seattle completing in March 2026. For people open to transit, particularly along the Bellevue to Redmond corridor, this is worth factoring into your thinking. The Redmond Technology Station sits on the Microsoft campus and is connected to the west campus by a pedestrian bridge.

FAQ

Common Questions

Is it realistic to bike commute to Microsoft's Redmond campus from nearby neighborhoods?

Yes, from parts of Redmond and even southern Kirkland it's genuinely doable. The Sammamish River Trail connects to the campus area via Marymoor Park, and Microsoft has strong internal infrastructure for cyclists including covered parking and showers. It works best if you're within three to five miles of campus and comfortable with the trail grade. From Sammamish or Bellevue, biking is much harder to make practical.

Does it matter which school district a Sammamish address falls under?

Yes, it can matter quite a bit. Sammamish is served by three school districts: Lake Washington School District, Issaquah School District, and Snoqualmie Valley School District. All are well regarded, but the specific schools your kids would attend and the district boundaries are worth verifying directly with the relevant district before you're under contract. Don't assume based on the city name alone.

What is the difference in price between living in Redmond versus Kirkland near the Microsoft campus?

It varies by property type, but generally speaking, comparable single family homes in Kirkland, especially near the waterfront or downtown, tend to run higher than similar homes in Redmond. The gap narrows when you move to North Kirkland or Juanita. Bellevue spans the widest range of any city, from relatively affordable to among the most expensive on the Eastside.

Should I factor in the light rail when deciding where to buy?

Yes, and the picture is clearer now than it was a couple of years ago. Sound Transit's 2 Line is fully open, connecting Redmond Technology Station (on the Microsoft campus) to Bellevue and Seattle. If you're buying near a station along that corridor, transit access is a real, present benefit rather than a future promise. It's particularly relevant for buyers in Bellevue or along the east side of Lake Washington who want an alternative to driving.

What is the tradeoff between Woodinville and somewhere like Redmond or Kirkland for a Microsoft employee?

Woodinville gives you more land, a quieter setting, and generally lower prices per square foot than Kirkland or central Redmond. The cost is commute time and the fact that it's less connected overall. If you're in office three to five days a week, most people find it starts to wear. If you're hybrid at two days or fewer, it's a reasonable trade for the extra space and lifestyle it offers.
